# Peer Feedback Workshop

> What could you learn about your project by seeing it through someone else's eyes?

Before the formal critique, we will share **work in progress** and help each other find useful next steps. You do not need a finished project or a polished presentation. The aim is to **explore possibilities**, not to evaluate or defend the work.

We will work in pairs for **two feedback rounds**, with a different partner each time. You will both give and receive feedback. Afterwards, you will have time to develop your project.

## 1. Prepare something to show (10 min)

Open your current project, prototype, sketches, experiments or code. Even an unfinished idea is fine, as long as there is something concrete to look at.

Prepare **one question you would like help with**. For example:

- Is the idea behind my project understandable?
- Which of these variations feels worth developing?
- What could I simplify or make more interesting?
- Does the interaction contribute to the concept?

You only need a short introduction. Do not prepare slides.

## 2. Give and receive feedback

We will use three simple ways of responding:

| I notice... | I wonder... | What if... |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What catches your attention? What is already working or interesting? | What is unclear? What would you like to understand better? | What is one direction or experiment worth trying? |

**Describe what you see before suggesting changes.** Ask questions before giving solutions. Your task is to help the other person see new possibilities, not redesign their project for them.

### One exchange: 12 minutes per student

| Time | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 3 min | **Show:** Explain your work and ask your feedback question. |
| 3 min | **Notice and wonder:** Your peer describes what they see and asks questions. |
| 4 min | **Explore:** Discuss one or two possible directions using "What if...?" |
| 2 min | **Capture:** Note what you found useful or want to investigate. |

Then **swap roles** and repeat. Both students should get the full 12 minutes.

### Two rounds, different partners

**Round 1:** Work with your assigned partner. Each person takes a turn (24 minutes total).

**Round 2:** We will shuffle the pairings so everyone meets **someone new**. Repeat the same exchange (24 minutes total). You do not need to show a different project.

The teacher will organise the pairings. [Pair shuffler: planned tool](#pair-shuffler-planned).

## 3. Decide what to explore (10 min)

Work individually again. Look at your notes and decide:

1. What was the most useful or surprising feedback?
2. What is **one concrete change, question or experiment** you want to try?
3. Is there any suggestion you do **not** want to follow? Why?

You are not required to accept every suggestion. The important thing is making a deliberate decision.

## 4. Studio time (about 55 min)

Use the remaining work period to **test or develop one idea** from the feedback. You might change a rule, adjust a parameter, try a variation, simplify the concept or prototype a different interaction.

You don't need to complete the change today. Save whatever you tried, including ideas that did not work.

## 5. Close and document

Keep a short note in your **Obsidian process journal**:

- the question you brought to the feedback session
- one or two useful observations or suggestions
- what you chose to test, change or keep, and why
- your next step before the critique

A few lines, images or screenshots are enough. No separate feedback report is needed.

**This week:** What could I explore next?  
**Next week's critique:** What have I developed, and how well does it communicate my intention?

---

## Facilitator notes

A possible **150-minute rhythm** is 10 minutes introduction, 10 minutes preparation, 24 minutes first feedback round, 5 minutes to change partners, 24 minutes second round, 10 minutes individual takeaways, 55 minutes studio work and 12 minutes closing and transitions.

Keep the introduction short. Project questions and the three feedback prompts do most of the teaching. Students should spend the majority of the time talking to peers or working on their own project.
