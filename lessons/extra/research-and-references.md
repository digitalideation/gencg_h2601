# Research & References: From Inspiration to Experimentation

> Research is not just collecting things you like. It can help you understand an idea, discover possibilities and decide what to try next.

In this workshop, we will look at how artists document their thinking, follow a live research demonstration, then investigate references for our own projects.

You don't need a finished project, a long essay or a large collection of links. **One or two references that you understand and can use are enough.**

## 1. Two ways to document creative work

### Vera Molnár: thinking through experiments

Vera Molnár's notebooks show how drawing, repeating, varying and comparing ideas can be part of artistic thinking. A process journal does not have to show only successful or polished results. Sketches, incomplete ideas and unexpected variations matter too.

- [Vera Molnár: journal, part 1](../../assets/pdf/vera_journal_01.pdf)
- [Vera Molnár: journal, part 2](../../assets/pdf/vera_journal_02.pdf)

**Look for:** How can a series of small variations reveal a question worth exploring?

### Zach Lieberman: connecting research to a project

In [Más Que la Cara: Overview](https://zachlieberman.medium.com/m%C3%A1s-que-la-cara-overview-48331a0202c0), Zach Lieberman documents an interactive public artwork developed with collaborators. The article connects artistic influences, a collection of mask references, workshops, graphical experiments and technical choices.

This is a much larger project than yours. **You are not expected to document your work at this scale.** Look at how references and experiments inform decisions, not at how long the article is.

### Investigate the article (15 min)

Read the introduction and the **Design process** section. Skim the technical details only if they interest you.

Find **one example of each**:

1. **An artistic reference:** What inspired the visual direction?
2. **An experiment:** What did the team try or test?
3. **A problem:** What did they need to figure out?
4. **A decision:** What direction did they choose, and why?

Point to a sentence, sketch or image that supports each answer. Then compare a few discoveries together.

**Question to keep in mind:** What is the difference between collecting a reference and actually using it?

## 2. A simple research blueprint

You can use this process whenever you start a project or get stuck during development.

### A. Start with a question

Choose something you want to understand. It can be visual, conceptual or technical.

For example: *How can typography become interactive?* This could lead you to investigate text that moves, stretches or responds to a cursor.

A specific question helps you search more purposefully than *I need some inspiration*.

### B. Discover and investigate

Search for artists, projects, techniques and processes. Try different keywords. Open the **artist's or project's original page** when possible, rather than relying on a repost, a moodboard or an AI-generated summary.

Ask yourself:

- What does this work do, and what interests me about it?
- Can I find information about its process, not only its final image?
- Is it relevant to my question?

An artistic reference can inform the idea, behaviour or visual language of your work. A technical reference can help you understand how to implement something. Both are useful.

### C. Collect and annotate

Keep the information you will need later:

- **Artist or creator, work title and year** (when available)
- **A link to the original source**
- **An image, sketch or screenshot** when you are allowed to reuse it, with a clear credit
- **A short observation** about the work or its process

If you are not sure whether an artwork image can be republished in your public journal, link to the original work instead of copying it.

### D. Connect it to your own project

A reference becomes useful when you explain the connection.

Instead of only writing *I like these letters*, try something more specific:

*The letters shift away from the mouse, but the word stays readable. In my sketch, I could use the cursor's distance from each letter to control how far it moves.*

You can also question or reject an approach. Research does not mean copying a style.

### E. Test something and reflect

Choose **one small experiment or decision** suggested by the research. It could be a p5.js sketch, a drawing, a different parameter, a change in interaction or a new question.

Record what you tried, or what you plan to test next. You do not need to finish it today.

**Question → Discover → Collect → Connect → Test → Reflect**

## 3. Put it in your process journal

Add your research to your **existing weekly entry in Obsidian**. You can use the short template below for each relevant reference. Adapt or shorten it as needed.

~~~markdown
### Reference: Artist - Work title (year)

**Source:** [Original project page](https://example.com)
**Image:** Add a credited image if you may reuse it, or link to the image.

**What I notice:** What is specific or interesting about this work?

**Connection to my project:** Why does it matter for my idea?

**What I could try:** One experiment, variation or decision.
~~~

This is **not** a new mandatory journal format. It fits within the existing [process journal guidance](./journal.md), which also asks you to document experiments, algorithmic thinking and reflection.

## 4. Research your own project

After the live demonstration, use the following checkpoints:

1. **Research (25 min):** Start from a question about your project. Find one or two promising references. Follow the original sources and investigate how the work was made.
2. **Document (20 min):** Add them to your journal with source links, observations and a connection to your project. Use the template if helpful.
3. **Explore (30 min):** Test one idea or describe a specific next experiment. It is fine if the experiment remains unfinished.
4. **Close (10 min):** Be ready to share one reference, why it matters and what you intend to do next.

If you received useful peer feedback in the previous workshop, you can use one of those questions as your starting point.

## What to leave with

By the end of the workshop, your journal should show:

- **one research question**
- **one or two well-chosen references**, with proper sources
- **your own observations and connection** to your project
- **one experiment, test or concrete next step**

You are not being asked to build a moodboard or write an academic paper. Show how research influences your creative decisions.
