# AI and Creativity

> What do we learn, choose and share when AI becomes part of creative work?

We have already worked with rules, chance and systems that surprise us. We have designed conditions for something to happen, looked at the results and changed our minds. AI brings another set of possibilities into this process. It also raises questions about what we learn, who has influence and how creative work connects us to other people.

## How we use this page

Each section begins with something to **try, inspect or compare**. Start there in class. The background that follows gives us ideas to connect to our observations and material to return to afterwards. Sections can be used across different sessions.

Bring an existing experiment, with or without AI, and an earlier version if you have one. A screenshot, sketch, piece of code or short description is enough. You can also use an example supplied by the lecturer or our [drawing-machine references](../lesson4_behaviour/drawing-machines.md). When a process is unknown, say so.

Our rhythm is:

1. Think, look or make a small note on your own.
2. Compare with a partner or small group.
3. Share a few different observations. The lecturer connects these to the ideas below.
4. Keep one insight, changed decision or open question.

You can think and take notes in any language. Drawing, pointing and short phrases are welcome. Speaking to the whole class is optional. These are working notes for your existing journal, not extra submissions. Follow the [AI mode of your current activity](../../readme.md#ai-in-this-course); the discussion does not require a new AI account or live generation.

## 1. Making and learning

**What do we discover by making something?**

### Start with two versions

**Do, individually then in pairs:**

1. Look at an earlier and a later version of an experiment. Mark one change that came from testing, an unexpected result or someone's feedback.
2. Show your partner what changed. Explain what you understood afterwards that you did not understand before.
3. Together, identify a difficulty you would want help with and a part you would want to explore yourself. Explain your reasons.

If you only have one version, sketch a possible change and say what you would hope to learn by trying it.

**Keep:** One observation about what helped your understanding change.

### Learning through attempts and encounters

An idea can change when we draw, run a sketch or test a rule. For example, your drawing machine makes an unexpected loop. You change one rule, compare the versions and decide that the loop is worth keeping. The original plan has changed through making.

The [Human Learning guide](https://github.com/digitalideation/hslu-ki-h2601/blob/main/content/human-learning.md) introduces Charles Pépin's philosophical account of learning through failure, other people and culture. A mistake alone does not explain what we learned. Feedback, attention, practice and a change of approach may help us understand it.

The [Perception guide](https://github.com/digitalideation/hslu-ki-h2601/blob/main/content/perception-guide.md) brings another perspective through Merleau-Ponty: our involvement with the world helps shape what we notice and understand. In creative practice, a gesture, a material or a rhythm can reveal a possibility while we work. This gives us a way to discuss our experience of making.

AI might remove a technical or language barrier that prevents someone from developing an idea. It might also let someone skip an encounter through which their idea could change. Effort can help us learn, but difficulty is not automatically valuable. Ask what this particular way of working makes possible and what it leaves out.

**Bring back to the class:** Compare a case where help opened a possibility with one where working through a difficulty was useful. The lecturer can connect the examples to learning through feedback and experience. Which part would you approach differently next time?

## 2. Agency

**Who shapes the choices?**

### Trace the decisions

**Do, in pairs:**

1. Return to your experiment. Identify a decision you made, a choice shaped by the tool and something about the process you cannot explain yet. Label them on the work or in a small diagram.
2. Choose one of those decisions. Could you change it, reject it or propose another option? What would you need in order to do that?
3. Consider someone else affected by the work: a viewer, collaborator or person whose material you used. Choose a decision they might want a say in. Compare it with the maker's view.

For work without AI, consider the rules, library or interface you used. You can then discuss what might change if you brought an AI system into that process. Keep actual observations and imagined possibilities clear.

**Keep:** One decision you could influence and one you would like to question, with a reason.

### Choosing, changing and refusing

Agency means having influence over what happens. It includes choosing an option, suggesting another, changing the question or saying no. We can examine how much influence someone has at different points in a process.

Imagine a model gives your image smoother forms. You like the result, but the rough texture mattered to your idea. You can keep the texture, explore the smoother version or look for another approach. Changing your intention can be a creative decision too. What led you there?

Some decisions happen before the result appears: which question we ask, what material we use, which tool we choose and what counts as a useful result. Others are made by people who design the system or decide how it is offered. We may understand some of these conditions and have little information about others.

The [Creativity and Society guide](https://github.com/digitalideation/hslu-ki-h2601/blob/main/content/creativity-and-society.md#further-exploration-optional) asks when AI supports our choices and when we mainly accept options shaped elsewhere. We can investigate that in a particular experiment. An unexpected suggestion might expand our choices; it might also lead us away from something we cared about.

### From personal choice to democratic participation

Agency also concerns decisions we share. This opens questions about democracy: who can participate, whose experience is heard, who sets the rules and how those rules can be challenged.

AI might help someone express an idea in an unfamiliar language. That could open a way to contribute. We can then ask who gets to hear the work, who receives recognition and who can influence the conditions for sharing it. Access to making, access to an audience and influence over shared decisions deserve separate attention.

For example, if a class presents work in a shared exhibition, who decides how AI contributions are described? How can makers, viewers and people whose material was used explain their concerns? A vote is one possible decision method. The group still has to decide which options are considered and how to respond to disagreement.

**Bring back to the class:** Compare two decisions that sit with different people. The lecturer can distinguish choosing within a system from influencing its conditions. What would give the people affected more influence?

## 3. Collaboration

**How do we create meaning together?**

### Let someone else interpret the work

**Do, in pairs:**

1. Show your work before explaining what it means. Your partner chooses a word, sketch or gesture for their response, then points to something in the work that led them there.
2. Explain your intention. Compare the two readings. What did your partner notice that you had not considered?
3. Swap roles. Each person then chooses one thing to revise, test or deliberately keep, and explains why.

For a course example, compare your interpretations and suggest a change you would explore. You do not need to guess the artist's intention.

If AI contributed to your work, identify what it suggested or produced and how people responded. Otherwise, name a point where you might invite an AI contribution. Treat that as a possibility to investigate.

**Keep:** One contribution from another person that changed your understanding, including a disagreement you want to keep exploring.

### Working with different experiences

Collaboration means working together so that other contributions can change the work. We bring experiences, languages and references. Listening, explaining and disagreeing can help an idea develop. A shared project also asks us to discuss what we are trying to do and who is responsible for which decisions.

You might see the movement in your sketch as playful while a classmate finds it tense. Their response could lead you to reconsider the rhythm. It could also help you understand why you want to keep it. Collaboration can leave room for different readings.

### Symbols, culture and shared meaning

A symbol is a word, image, sound or gesture that stands for an idea. What it means can depend on who encounters it and in what situation. We draw on things we have learned together: language, stories, images, conventions and memories. These help us communicate, while leaving room for misunderstanding and new interpretations.

The source guide asks what happens when the production of this symbolic material becomes automated. A generated image might help someone express an experience that was difficult to communicate. A familiar phrase might make that experience easier to understand, or remove something particular about it. These are possibilities to examine in actual work, rather than conclusions about every AI output.

Creating also helps shape the world we share. A story can make an experience visible. An image can question a familiar way of seeing. We can ask whose experiences enter that conversation and whose are missing. This connects collaboration back to agency: who can contribute to the meanings through which people understand each other?

### What do we mean by an AI collaborator?

We can name the role we are inviting AI to play: generating alternatives, finding a technical problem, suggesting a description or questioning an idea. Then we can ask what that contribution changes.

The source's emphasis on listening, patience and effort gives us another question. What happens when a contribution challenges what we wanted? A classmate may bring concerns and experiences we did not anticipate. An AI suggestion can also prompt us to reconsider an approach. We need to examine the exchange: what changed, who could respond and who takes responsibility?

Calling a system a collaborator opens a discussion about suggestions, shared purpose, interpretation and responsibility. It does not settle how human and machine contributions should be understood. The [source guide](https://github.com/digitalideation/hslu-ki-h2601/blob/main/content/creativity-and-society.md#further-exploration-optional) offers these questions for investigation.

**Bring back to the class:** Share two different interpretations of one work. The lecturer can connect the difference to context and shared meaning. Which contribution changed a decision, and what remained unresolved?

## 4. Value and trust

**Why might the process matter to us?**

### Compare two perspectives

**Start with this imagined situation:** You enjoy a short animation. You then learn that its maker used AI to generate some images, selected parts and edited the sequence. The animation itself has not changed.

**Do, individually then in groups of three or four:**

1. Write or mark your first response. Did anything change in how you value the work? Name what you would want to know.
2. Choose two perspectives from the table below. Explain what each person might value and find one possible disagreement. You can hold a different view yourself.
3. Consider one more detail: would your response differ if the maker had explained the AI use from the beginning, or had previously claimed that every image was drawn by hand?

These are fictional situations for discussion, not claims about anyone's actual work.

| Perspective | What might matter? |
| --- | --- |
| Maker | Recognition for ideas, selection, editing and other decisions |
| Viewer | The experience of the work and trust in its description |
| Another creator | Whether their contribution or influence is recognised |
| Exhibition organiser | How to present the work and explain its process to an audience |

**Keep:** One disagreement and the information that might help the people discuss it. Agreement is not required.

### Quality, trust and recognition

People may value the result, the maker's intention, the experience behind it or the effort involved. These can matter differently to each person. A viewer may still enjoy a work while questioning how it was described. A maker may feel that their editing and judgment have been overlooked.

It helps to distinguish enjoying the work, trusting the account of its creation and deciding who deserves credit. Knowing that AI was involved does not answer all three questions. We need to look at what happened and why it matters to the people involved.

The [Creativity and Society guide](https://github.com/digitalideation/hslu-ki-h2601/blob/main/content/creativity-and-society.md#used-cars-and-trust-the-lemons-analogy) uses a used-car analogy to explain unequal information. A seller may know more about a car than a buyer. Uncertainty can make buyers suspicious even of reliable cars. Similarly, creators and audiences may know different amounts about a creative process. Suspicion can affect people who have described their work honestly too.

The comparison concerns information and trust. AI assistance does not make a creative work the equivalent of a defective car. Artistic value and mechanical condition are different questions. A clear account of a process gives people more information for their judgment; it need not make everyone judge the work in the same way.

**Bring back to the class:** Share a disagreement between two perspectives. The lecturer can ask whether it concerns quality, trust, credit or several of these. What information would help, and which difference in values might remain?

## 5. Our own practice

**What does this mean for the work we make?**

Return to the experiment or example you started with. Look again at your first observation and the decisions you marked.

**Do:** Choose one thing to revisit after the discussion: a rule, a tool choice, an interpretation, a credit or an explanation of the process. Make a small revision if practical, or name a specific next action and why you want to try it.

Show your partner what changed in your thinking. They can ask for an example or point out something still unclear.

If useful, keep a short note with the existing evidence in your [process journal](./journal.md):

- At first I thought...
- This observation or contribution made me reconsider...
- I will change, test or keep... because...

An unresolved question is a useful outcome too. Explain what you would need to explore it.
